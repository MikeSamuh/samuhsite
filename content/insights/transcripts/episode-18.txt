In episode 14, I talked about how well-being drives performance and our research which shows
four factors that organizations can actually influence or control. Healthy habits, autonomy,
purpose, and belonging that drive up well-being and therefore performance. Today, I want to talk
about one less understood dimension and that is autonomy. But first, click like and subscribe.
Let's start first by defining autonomy. Essentially, autonomy is three things. First,
it is flexibility over your time. Meaning, if my work timings are such that I can't pick up or drop
my kids from school or tend to an elderly parent or take care of my pet, it can cause me a great deal
of stress. Second, it's about flexibility over how to accomplish your work. Or in other words,
professional latitude. What that means is being either told what to do or how to do it, but not
both. Because all of us aspire to leave our unique mark on our work. And third, it is about having a
reasonable workload. It's fine to have stretch goals and in fact, it could even motivate us to perform at
a high level. But when they become unreasonable or we have unrelentingly long work hours. That is a
recipe for chronic stress, for ill health, for lack of well-being and therefore a drop in performance.
autonomy is about not suppressing or subsuming the individual within the organization. It is about the
ability to express one's talent freely and fully in service of the organization or team goals. Yet,
organizations are often apprehensive about giving autonomy. There's a fear that by doing so,
it could lead to chaos. But being free is not the same as free for all. Autonomy is always within the
context of boundaries. Just as when we live in a free society, we are still bound by the rules of the
society so we can all live in harmony with each other. And the same thing applies to organizations.
having autonomy for people within broad boundaries is actually a recipe for creating greater well-being
and greater performance.

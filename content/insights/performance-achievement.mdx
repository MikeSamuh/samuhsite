## The problem with performance management

In the 1980s Jack Welch single handedly changed the employment experience of millions of workers around the world. He introduced the forced ranking system within GE, a World War II era US Army practice used to enlist soldiers, and directed it for a different purpose altogether. He began to stratify people into performance categories (A, B and C players), with the intention of rewarding the best (A players) and exiting the C players. This practice, often referred to as the bell curve, soon spread across much of US and internationally, and it is estimated that by early 2000s, 60 percent of Fortune 500 had adopted some form of a forced ranking system.

Here's the problem. It is a fundamentally flawed practice.

1. The bell curve as an indicator of organizational performance has no basis in science. Josh Bersin's article in Forbes, aptly titled "The myth of the bell curve", demonstrated that the power curve better represented actual organizational performance. In any time horizon, a few people make extraordinarily strong contributions, while there was a "long tail" along which contributions of most people fall.
2. Performance ratings are highly demotivating and detract from high performance. In fact, it is the most disliked practice by employees around the world. One would argue that the purpose of a performance management system should be to raise individual and collective performance. In reality, it achieves the very opposite.
3. Organizational performance is a team sport, yet performance management is engineered to reward and penalize individuals. It takes teams of people to work in tandem to effectively achieve an organization's goals. Delivering at high levels of excellence requires each individual to operate at their best while collaborating with others. It calls on people to place the overarching mission above their personal agenda. However, the fear of a bad rating, juxtaposed against the rewards associated with a good one, drives people to optimize for themselves rather than their organization.

It is incredulous that despite all these grave issues, companies have struggled to come up with a better answer.

## The five universal truths of performance

In 2015, I was tasked to lead the overhaul of Accenture's performance management system. Scores of companies had begun to yank performance ratings in response to a global outcry from their employees, and we too sought a better way for ours. We started our quest with a 90 day "research sprint" to answer a singular question: what creates great individual and team performance? Our small, yet mighty team partnered with leading experts. They studied diverse environments within and outside the corporate domain, seeking to glean underlying principles for sustained high performance. We finally converged in a tiny conference room in our NYC office and began to make sense of it all. To our surprise, we found no correlation between the existence of a robust performance management system and organizational performance. Its primary purpose was to provide a rubric for pay decisions. The compensation tail was wagging the performance dog. What would happen if we snipped the metaphoric tail, I wondered?

Through our research, we gleaned five principles for high performance. They became the basis for our new paradigm. We called it Performance Achievement. We learnt that great performance happens when:

1. We do what we love and are great at. That is, we apply our skills and strengths, doing work that we are passionate about.
2. We focus on the vital few priorities. Not our goal sheets or key performance indicators, but the few aspects with the highest impact.
3. Performance is a team sport. When people complement each others' strengths and skills, have aligned priorities, and have a high trust equation.
4. Feedback is not an event. Annual appraisals, set in the rear view mirror, only serve to increase anxiety, not drive performance. Feedback needs to be woven into the fabric of daily work to help us get better at what we do.
5. Ratings and rankings detract from performance rather than enable it. Replacing these labels and numbers with hyper-personalized actions unleashes the ability for people to grow and contribute to the organization in distinctive ways.

The research sprint left us grappling with two big questions. How could we bring these principles to life? How would we pay our people?

## A renewed solution

Turning findings from a research sprint into an implementable solution was not a trivial undertaking. Nor was changing a decades old mindset in a gigantic, multi-national organization like ours. We recognized that the barriers were many and it would take a few years to bring this to life, one pilot at a time. On July 21, 2015, two weeks after I had moved bag, baggage, wife and dog from Singapore to New York, an unintended and premature comment by our CEO, Pierre Nanterme, during an interview in Washington Post went viral. "In big move, Accenture will get rid of annual performance reviews and rankings", screamed the headline. In an instant, 375,000 employees, our 1000 plus clients, and the industry at large heard the news. The same time as I did. The horse had bolted the stable.

Eventually, Pierre's faux-pas turned out to be a blessing in disguise. Now there was no turning back. We were going to reinvent performance management, implement it around the world within a year, and get it right for our people.

The five truths formed the bedrock of our solution and we led with a strengths based approach. Leveraging Gallup's capabilities, we provided our employees and new joiners with access to the StrengthsFinder assessment. They learnt about their strengths, non-strengths (Gallup abhors weakness), and how to leverage each other more effectively. We deployed a team engagement tool globally. An ineffective and clunky organization-wide engagement survey was replaced by a quick and empowering tool which gave teams valuable insights to enhance the experience of their members. We scrapped the age old practice of setting annual goals and instead guided people to write out no more than five to seven priorities. In a constantly evolving business context, we expected people to pivot their focus as conditions changed, rather than stay anchored to a rigid annual objective. Each of these elements, strengths, engagement, and priorities, was designed to trigger a dialogue with peers as well as bosses. Feedback needed to be in the moment, and not as an event. The biggest change was removing ratings. We introduced personalized "talent actions". Actions included promoting those performing above their level of responsibility; identifying avenues for people to continue to grow their skills; changing roles for those who needed a new challenge; and honestly dialoguing with (the very few) who we did not see thriving with us on transitioning to a new company. The dreaded ratings dog-fight finally gave way to a dialogue focused on supporting people.

## How to pay people without ratings

I have often been asked to share my perspective on performance management with Chief HR Officers, business leaders and in industry forums. Invariably, the big question on people's minds is: how do you pay people without ratings? In reality, it is a rather simple problem to solve.

In most organizations, executives make two primary decisions on compensation, base pay and bonus. At senior levels, there may be a third decision on awarding equity. In the world of performance ratings, these decisions get conflated and the rating formulaically drives all three decisions. This practice has shortcomings and we need to separate each decision.

Let's start with base pay. Base pay is associated with a role. Each role commands a different price in the market. If we pay people below market, they will leave us and we will also not be able to attract the right talent. Paying people above market would mean overpricing the roles. Thus, base pay has everything to do with the market and very little with performance. Benchmarking base pay to the market and paying people in like roles similarly is both practical and perceived to be fair.

The second decision is on allocating bonus. Unlike base pay, bonus has much to do with results, and very little with the market. This is where the power curve comes in. In any time horizon, let's say a year, we can look at people (or teams) in terms of their impact on the business. Some would be "hyper" contributors having driven significant results for the organization, many others would fall along a long tail, and a few may have fallen short of expectations. Logic would dictate allocating larger bonus amounts to the first group and proportionately less for others along the curve. For those that missed their mark, well, giving them a bonus would be (an undeserved) gift.

Finally, how do we allocate the few precious equity awards to people? We might apply the same reasoning as we do while deciding whether to invest in a stock. We buy shares in companies who show promise of high returns in the future. Similarly, we ought to give equity to those who demonstrate strong potential for significant future contribution. Often, these may be the same ones that are making high contributions currently. Yet, several factors impact performance in the short term, such as prevailing market conditions and maturity of the product or offering. Some, who are delivering strong results today, may not have similar capacity to do so in the future.

Guided by these principles, leaders can work together to make fair and equitable pay decisions for their people.

## Finding your ikigai

Hector Garcia and Francesc Miralles set out to understand secrets of longevity in the five Blue Zones, regions in the world where people live the longest. They zoomed in on Okinawa, a prefecture in Japan, which occupies first place. Their famous book, Ikigai, beautifully captures the ancient Japanese concept which loosely translates to "the reason we get out of bed every morning", our raison d'être. Their discovery, coming after interviewing some of the longest living people on earth, put a spotlight on the keys to a happy and long life: staying active, eating in a balanced and mindful way, doing low-intensity exercises, learning to persevere through difficulties, keeping a positive mindset, the importance of reducing stress, sleeping, and crucially, nurturing friendships throughout one's life.

Helping people find their ikigai is a starting point to committing to human expansion. This can be translated into practice in simple ways. Let's take recruiting as an example. Most organizations recruit based on some rubric of education and experience. For example, the ranking of one's school, academic performance, and past achievements form the basis upon which we decide a person's ability to do a job. In reality, none of these are accurate markers for predicting one is fit for a job.

## Potential over pedigree

More often than not, we get seduced by a person's pedigree and fail to see their potential. Recruiting for potential would lead us down a path of dismantling current standards and norms. First, we would seek to understand the unique gifts each person has to offer. Their strengths, skills, and passion. Second, rather than going through an interview process, we would get recruits and their prospective team mates in their natural habitat trying out real work activities together, so that the right decision would emerge organically. It would give ample opportunity for hiring managers to see the skills and strengths of the potential recruit, who would in turn experience the organizational culture and environment first hand. This collaborative process will prevent costly mistakes. Finally, our job descriptions would simply lay out the work to be done, the context in which the organization operates, and remove requirements for unnecessary educational qualifications or grades, which may have no correlation with the actual work content.

Many organizations have an obsession for hiring people from top tier institutions, largely the result of generations of leaders who have come from such backgrounds. Research in most countries shows a high correlation between family income and the demographics of these institutions. Moving away from a mindset of pedigree opens doors for talented and driven people from a broader cross section of society into the workforce.

It is a win on multiple levels. Companies get access to a wider pool of talent bringing down often exorbitant salary levels driven by perceived talent scarcity. More people do what they love and have an aptitude for, resulting in higher performance, increased retention, and better work environments with lower stress and burnout. As more of the society partakes in organizational work, income levels rise more equitably, which begins to bend the curve of rising inequality.

It is a simple equation. When we consider potential over pedigree, more people live their ikigai, thus proving that what is good for one can be good for many.

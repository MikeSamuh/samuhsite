## The current landscape: mental health, engagement, and mind health

With the prevalence of mental distress on the rise around the world, especially in younger generations, mental health and wellbeing has increasingly become a topic of concern for employers. As a result, workplace mental wellbeing programs such as mindfulness apps and therapy have increasingly gained traction across companies.

However, some studies suggest that they do not work. For example, a study of 32,974 employees at a large warehouse retail company in the United States found that while employees exposed to a workplace wellness program reported significantly more positive health behaviors, there were no significant effects on clinical measures of health, health care spending and utilization, or employment outcomes after 18 months. Similarly, a review of survey data from 46,336 workers across 233 organizations in the United Kingdom found employees who participated in a range of common individual-level wellbeing interventions, including resilience training, mindfulness and wellbeing apps were no better off across multiple indicators of subjective wellbeing.

In efforts divorced from initiatives under the bucket of mental health, various companies focus on engagement, looking at things like friendships at work, trust in management and so on as a means to boost productivity and reduce attrition.

Yet mental health and engagement are all behaviors of the mind. The question is, how are they related and are they simply two sides of the same coin? We show that when we measure mind health in its entirety, work culture may be one of the dominant causes of mental health challenges demanding an integrated and unified approach.

Rather than inadvertently creating a culture that causes or exacerbates mental health problems and then providing therapy and apps to counter it, companies could become more strategic about creating a culture of mind health for sustained high performance.

## Measure what matters: assessing mind health

We define mind health as the ability to navigate life's challenges and function productively. This necessarily calls on all of the capabilities of mind.

While mental health assessments typically assess problems and negative feelings, they do not assess strengths and functional capacity. Similarly, engagement tools typically assess perceptions of the external environment rather than an individual's capability.

Here we describe an assessment called the MHQ or Mind Health Quotient that evaluates 47 aspects of mental function spanning the dimensions of Mood and Outlook, Social Self (the relational aspects of our function), Adaptability and Resilience, Drive and Motivation, Cognition and Mind-Body Connection (the physical consequences of mental challenges). These 47 aspects include both mental health symptoms as well as the positive aspect of mental function and are queried on a life-impact scale. The score ranges from -100 to +200 and is categorized from distressed to thriving.

The assessment, which typically is taken anonymously, delivers an aggregate score as well as scores along the dimensions above. For takers it can also provide a detailed personalized report with self-help guidance providing a motivation to complete it.

The MHQ score at an aggregate level has been shown to relate linearly to productive days (presenteeism) and non-linearly to days absent (absenteeism) providing a read out of not just feeling but also function.

## Mind health and work culture

The Global Mind Project, which uses the MHQ, tracks trends across the world in 17 languages and 72 plus countries. With over 1.8 million respondents it is now the largest database of mind health profiles coupled with deep demographics and life context factors. This database is openly available to the research community.

Work culture is one of the life context factors probed in the Global Mind Project. Our research queried nine factors: relationship with colleagues, relationship with manager, pride and purpose in work, being informed about what is going on, workload, flexibility over time, flexibility over how you do your job, learning and growth opportunities, being valued and recognized.

Here is what we found:

- Having poor relationships with colleagues and a low sense of pride and purpose in one's work are associated with the biggest decreases in overall mind health, on par with having challenges with family relationships.
- Those who rated these factors poorly are more likely to have feelings of sadness and hopelessness, unwanted, strange thoughts, reduced energy, decreased drive and motivation, physical health issues and a sense of being detached from reality, even after controlling for other factors.
- Work-life balance factors such as workload and flexibility over time are also important, but less so. Excess workload, for instance, results in poor sleep, diminished self-worth, worse appetite regulation and even increased nightmares, while lack of flexibility over time had much smaller impacts on similar factors.
- Those working remotely fared worse, on average, than those working in person or hybrid. Similarly, those working alone fared worse, on average, than those in larger teams, and those in jobs involving customer service and physical labor fared worse than those in human care, knowledge work and business management jobs.
- The nine aspects of work culture that we looked at had a several-fold greater impact on mental wellbeing than aspects of work structure, such as whether one worked remotely or in person, alone or in a team and the type of job.

In the aggregate, the magnitude of impact was such that a poor experience of work on these dimensions could easily make the difference between good mind health and being in a place of mental distress associated with clinical mental health disorder diagnosis.

## Mind health in the world

The most dominant trend in the global internet-enabled world is a profoundly decreasing mind health with each younger generation, whereas prior to 2008, data on elements such as sadness and anxiety indicated that young people always fared best.

The magnitude is such that almost half of young adults 18 to 24 are in a range we consider distressed or struggling at a level that qualifies for clinical diagnosis.

The dimension that has declined the most is the Social Self, how people see themselves and are able to relate to others. This includes things like forming and maintaining positive relationships and being able to cooperate with others towards goals. These are fundamentally important for work. Also substantially diminished are Mood and Outlook, followed by Adaptability and Resilience and Cognition. All of these factors are profoundly important for work.

This young demographic (Gen Z) will form 40 percent of the global workforce within the next five to seven years, thus making it increasingly harder to manage for employers with their existing approaches.

## The challenges of work today

Building a work culture is intentional and encompasses every aspect of an employee life cycle from onboarding to performance management to their leaving.

These systems and processes can sometimes be misaligned with stated goals of work culture. Individual performance management, for example, can be opposed to the idea of well-functioning and integrated teams and can certainly create worse relationships that are detrimental to mind health.

Every organization is different depending on the demographics and their internal processes. Knowing where you stand on various dimensions is crucial. You cannot manage what you do not measure, and measure appropriately.

The social aspects are fundamental and are going to become far bigger challenges as younger generations join the workforce. Getting ahead of it now can make the difference between success and failure a decade from now.

Organizations that seek to consistently outperform others need to take a systemic approach towards mind health to achieve and sustain high levels of performance. This implies two things. First, they need to identify and address existing enterprise processes that impact mind health. Second, they need to take an integrated view across the organizational, group (team), and individual levels.

## Building a culture of mind health for sustained performance: the enterprise view

Measuring mind health and the impact of the above mentioned workplace factors will enable organizations to take an order of magnitude approach in identifying specific practices in their context that need to be changed.

As an example, the pervasive practice of performance management in the vast majority of organizations employs the use of a bell curve which rates individuals across different categories of performance, and determines their reward outcomes, career progression, and even their ability to remain employed. This is flawed at multiple levels. HR analyst Josh Bersin's research demonstrated how the bell curve was an incorrect representation of organizational performance. The far bigger issue is that this system causes a great deal of anxiety, creates internal competition and mistrust, and fails to acknowledge that organizational performance is most often the result of teams working together and not individual heroics or shortfalls. Despite path-breaking efforts by a few companies such as Accenture and Deloitte in revamping performance management, sadly this antiquated practice has remained unchanged in most others.

Other practices to review may be as simple as the food served in cafeterias and replacing ultra-processed foods with healthier choices given the significant detrimental impact on mind health as reported in our research. Similarly, enhancing opportunities for human-to-human interactions by replacing the convenience of easily accessible food vending machines with communal spaces to meet and eat, or increasing the proportion of collaboration spaces in offices relative to individual cubicles which would enhance teaming and creativity.

## The under-explored role of teams in enhancing mind health

Most organizational initiatives focus overwhelmingly on enterprise-wide processes, practices, and policies on the one end, and individual level interventions on the other. However, most people experience the work environment in the context of their team. Gallup's research over the years has shown how highly engaged teams are positively correlated with positive business outcomes like customer loyalty, sales and wellbeing. The team is thus where mind health can be best cultivated.

Empowering teams with data on their work practices can give them valuable insights on steps they can take to enhance their wellbeing and performance. Working with the leadership team of an ed-tech startup, we administered a three minute survey on these factors and shared the results in a heat map. This led to an animated discussion and the founder CEO decided to include a check-in question on workload in the standing calls with his direct reports.

Individual intentions in optimizing their wellbeing are unlikely to sustain if their immediate environments are not conducive. For example, being part of a globally distributed team comes with its fair share of calls at unearthly hours for people at the wrong end of the time zone. When teams can see the impact of work practices, it can prompt small, yet meaningful changes such as to call timings or attendance requirements, giving their members valuable time for rest and recovery.

## Ritualizing mind health practices: making change stick

Most organization transformation efforts incorporate change management techniques that involve behavior change training for leaders and managers. The effectiveness of such efforts is variable, and often times changes do not last beyond the duration of the interventions.

A vastly underused tactic in the corporate world is the time tested practice of rituals. Research has shown that when rituals are physical, psychological, and communal, they can enhance meaningfulness of work and better organizational citizenship behaviors.

Cultivating team rituals that are aligned with mind health in the flow of work can become the bedrock for sustaining high performance in organizations. A moment like joining a team can be hugely anxiety producing as the new person looks for acceptance and seeks to understand their role alongside other, established members. Ritualizing the simple practice of welcoming the new member and inviting the existing ones to share their stories instills a sense of belonging, provides clarity of role, and accelerates connection, all crucial factors to team performance.

Rituals are how culture has been passed through the course of human evolution. Rituals that enhance cohesion have been prevalent in tribes that have outlasted others over the centuries. In recent history, elite sports teams have adopted them to create a distinct identity and deepen belonging. The most successful franchise in sports history, the New Zealand All Blacks, are renowned for their pre-game haka, perhaps the most iconic of the many rituals that flow through their 120 year history. It is time the corporate world took a page from the world of sports.

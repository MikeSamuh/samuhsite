## Summary

Rahul traces performance management, the practice of rating people as high, average or low performers, to Jack Welch at General Electric, who borrowed it from a World War II US Army practice. Welch labelled the top 20 percent and the bottom 10 percent, rewarded the top with large bonuses and fired the bottom every year, and the practice spread worldwide. Rahul's case is that it is deeply flawed, dehumanising and without scientific basis. He gives three problems. The bell curve the ratings rest on is no indicator of organisational performance, and he cites Josh Bersin's research on the myth of the bell curve showing a few people or teams deliver disproportionate impact with a long tail behind them. Ratings demotivate, because being labelled a low performer sticks like a tattoo. And performance is a team sport, yet the system rewards and penalises individuals and pits teammates against each other.

## Key points

- Forced ranking came to GE from a wartime army practice and spread from there
- He cites ten in every hundred people, and their families, losing income under the practice
- The bell curve does not predict organisational performance; impact follows a long tail
- Labels like low performer demotivate rather than motivate
- Performance is a team sport, and individual ratings set teammates against each other
- A system meant to raise performance ends up lowering it

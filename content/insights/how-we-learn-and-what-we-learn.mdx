## How we learn

About a decade ago, I was serving as Accenture's Chief Learning Officer. It was a pivotal time for our company marked by significant geographic expansion, a rapidly changing business and technology landscape, and a compelling need to continuously up-skill our 350,000 people. With a learning budget of almost $1 billion, this was both a fiscal responsibility and a unique opportunity to re-imagine learning at scale. We approached this challenge with a first principles mindset, seeking to understand the latest brain science on how humans learn. Marrying developments in neuroscience with advancements in technology, we created inventions such as digital learning boards, connected classrooms around the world, and a network of global learning centers to meet the needs of our ever-growing workforce. It was during this exploration that we came across principles of learning laid out in ancient Indian texts, the Vedas. Translated somewhat loosely, the three pillars of Vedic learning are Sravana (instruction, to hear the teachings from a guru or expert), Manana (introspection, contemplating and reflecting upon the teachings), and Nididhyasana (immersion, continuous practice and application of the teachings). These pillars have universal applicability and form the fundamental architecture in education for centuries, as seen in the Gurukul system in India, the dojos of Japan, or apprenticeship models in the west in more recent times.

Stunning how science is catching up with ancient wisdom.

## What we learn

In Accenture we would often say that learning is in our DNA. It was deeply rooted in our psyche and the organization lived up to its commitment of continuous learning, regardless of economic cycles, giving it a well earned reputation as one of the foremost learning organizations in the world.

The curriculum was anchored around four skill dimensions: functional, technical, industry and professional. The specific mix of these skill areas were based on people's specializations, responsibilities, roles, and the business they belonged to, and would evolve as people progressed in their careers. Most organizations, especially in the knowledge economy, follow some variant of this approach.

I think of this as training for "cleverness", and have come to believe that focusing on exclusively developing the intellect is severely limiting. We have created a world of cleverer, but not healthier, wiser and kinder people. We need to broaden the realms for learning. For example:

Why would we not teach people how to access the innate intelligence resident in their body, so they could have long and healthy careers, not just productive years?

Ethics training covers organizations for legal exposure and risk. Why does it not teach people how to assess the impact of their products and services on people, society and the planet?

Driven by a fear of obsolescence, we obsess about staying current with every development in technology. Yet why is there no curriculum on "inner technology"? Such as learning to tune into the wisdom of our inner core or connecting with the intuition of our gut. This inner science, based on universal laws of nature, cultivated over thousands of years of evolution, and passed down the ages by our ancestors, is sadly lost, ignored or dismissed by a prevalent mindset that believes it knows better than those who came before them.

The result? Lopsided economic development that favors the privileged few at the expense of the vast majority. A hyper-connected digital world with ever increasing social disconnection, stress and mental illness. A world flooded with shiny gizmos and dazzling products that brings an entire planet to its knees in its desperate fight for survival.

It's high time we learn. The right things. The right way.

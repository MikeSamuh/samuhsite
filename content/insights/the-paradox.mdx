## From Rahul

As posted on samuh.work with this episode.

My mother hails from Bengal, a state in the east of India known for its rich history of literature, arts, cinema, economic and social sciences. It is the birthplace of many a literary and scientific giant. A unique aspect of Bengali culture is “adda”. Adda is “best described as an informal group conversation that’s long, fluid, and relaxed in nature”. It typically occurs within a “para”, or neighborhood. Adda is the edifice upon which their sense of community is built. A highlight of my teenage years was visiting my Bengali cousins who lived in a locality far from mine. Everyday after sunset, friends and neighbors would gather in their home, unannounced. Conversations would be organic, ranging from day-to-day occurrences to political discourse. As the evening progressed, banter would get more animated and sometimes vociferous. But always good natured and no grudges were long held.

Reflecting upon the unprecedented mental well-being crisis afflicting millions globally, I increasingly feel that we need to go beyond our current suite of solutions. Much of our focus has been on helping individuals cope with highly stressful and often isolated lives, resulting in a proliferation of apps, therapies and medications. These solutions are vital and need to continue to evolve. They also need to be more accessible.

Yet, I believe we need to go past the manifestation and look into the causes in which this malaise has arisen. Our goal cannot be the mere absence of illness. It ought to be fostering an ability to thrive. We are inherently a relational species. Our well-being is deeply interwoven in the fabric of society and our relationships. The unconditional love from our family, our friendships, and the trust that is built working “in the trenches” with co-workers are essential ingredients for leading healthy lives. Relationships that are forged in physical proximity. Not via Zoom. Not by texting. Not in VR. Not in the metaverse. In. Real. Life.

Just like Bengalis and their beloved adda.

## Summary

Rahul sets out a paradox. Mental health has been declining for over a decade, and healthcare companies, governments and organisations have responded with rising investment, yet the problem grows faster than the efforts to address it. His hypothesis is that most of that investment sits in what he calls the fix-the-person category: an app, a therapist, a drug therapy. He contends that this is not an individual problem but a societal one, rooted in the socio-commercial and technological landscape of the last several decades. He names two aspects, the rise of individualism and our relationship with technology. His suggestions are relational: families should talk rather than text and listen rather than advise, institutions should get intentional about community and gather locally and often, and individuals should measure what they treasure.

## Key points

- He cites US mental health spending rising three and a half to five percent a year, with one in five adults still suffering
- Most investment goes into fixing the person rather than the environment that produces the problem
- Two roots: rising individualism and our relationship with smartphones
- For families: talk rather than text, listen rather than advise, delay smartphones
- For organisations and schools: get intentional about community, gather locally and often, share stories, celebrate and commiserate together
- For individuals: take an assessment like the MHQ and act on it

Entitled or struggling?

That was how my conversation with the energetic and effervescent Mangalam Maloo began when he raised the topic of Gen Z in the workplace.

We had settled in for a chat at Soho House NYC and ordered matchas, my first, and certainly not my last, thanks to Mangalam.

This is a topic that often triggers a stereotypical generational debate. But beneath it lies a more important question: what might it be telling us about the unintended consequences of human progress?

Thanks to the pioneering work of our partners in Sapien Labs, we are beginning to see a phenomenon that leaders, educators, and employers need to pay attention to. At Samuh we call it **capacity to perform**: the aggregate of our cognitive, emotional, relational, and physical capacities that enable us to do our work, navigate life's challenges, and contribute meaningfully.

The trends are deeply concerning:

- Capacity to perform shows declines of 3 to 5x between Baby Boomers and Gen Z, with each intervening generation being lower than the one before
- **Social self** (our ability to build relationships and collaborate), **mood and outlook** (maintaining positivity in the face of adversity), and **cognition** (attention, planning, decision-making, and learning) show some of the steepest declines
- This is a widespread global phenomenon

Why might this be happening?

The research points to a complex interplay of factors, a few decades in the making: weakening family and community structures, increased smartphones leading to fewer human connections and more sedentary lifestyles, poorer nutrition, and increased environmental toxins.

For no fault of their own, many young people are inheriting the consequences of choices that were made before them. As MIT professor and Founder of Presencing Institute Otto Scharmer says, "We have created results that nobody wants."

Why does this matter?

Most individuals, teams, and organizations aspire to high performance. Yet **performance can never sustainably exceed capacity**. And capacity is not fixed. Research shows that capacity is impacted by our work environment, which in turn is shaped by our day-to-day work practices. How we set goals, measure progress, give and receive feedback, conduct meetings, onboard new colleagues, and support transitions forms our environment, which shapes our capacity, and ultimately creates performance.

The Gen Z conversation matters in its own right. Yet, it raises a broader one: *are we intentionally creating the conditions for people and toplines to thrive together?*

A conversation that I hope is guided by science and informed by wisdom. Where we, in corporate life, can learn from other disciplines and cultures, such as the world of team sport.

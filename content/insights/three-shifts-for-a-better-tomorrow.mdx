If there is one lesson from the great challenges we are confronted with, a profound crisis of mental health, vast and rising inequality, and a planet under siege, for me it is the need to honor connection. Connection with one another, with the society we reside in, with other sentient beings, and importantly a connection with one's higher self.

How might we foster such connection in the practical, tactical world of work?

I believe we need to effect three shifts:

1. From individual to team orientation
2. From globally dispersed, virtually connected to hyper-local, interdisciplinary, autonomous teams
3. From offices designed for work, to offices designed for creativity, collaboration, connection, and celebration

Let's explore each of these shifts.

## From individual islands to teams that ride the waves. Together.

Imagine a recruiting process involving extensive interaction with your future coworkers while performing real work together. Upon conclusion of this trial phase, you are approached by the team lead who sits with you and generously shares the unique gifts they saw in you and how honored they would be to have you on their team. You expectantly go for your first day in the office, which concludes with the team gathered in a circle. It is a ritual to welcome new joiners and each person shares their personal story, until finally you are invited to share yours.

You belong.

Next, you are given a curriculum. It has five dimensions of learning: skills, relationships, holistic health practices, ethics, and self-realization. While it is tailored to your role, the learning journey is a collective one, undertaken together as a team. Learning events are regularly woven into the weekly team schedule.

Goals are set for, and as, a team. They are multi-dimensional, meaning the team is measured on their output and financial metrics, as well as on collective wellbeing, and positive impact on the society and planet. Progress is visible to all on a dashboard. Milestones are regularly celebrated. Shortfalls trigger introspection as a group, invariably concluding with stories of overcoming past failures. Incentives are team based, and tied to their multi-dimensional goals.

We are on the same boat. We ride the waves together.

Exceptional individual efforts and contributions are praised by all. As are precious milestones like marriages and parenthood. Personal adversity is a shared burden and not a personal hell. Errors and personal shortfalls are not glossed over, yet treated with compassion as fellow members step in to coach and help.

You will never walk alone.

Shifting to a team orientation does not mean suppressing the individual. Rather, it is about each person being in the fullness of their capability and contribution. The team and organizational mission is above each member's personal aspiration, and can only be achieved through the fullest expression of their individual talent.

Transitions are sacred moments. Regardless of the circumstances, the departing person's journey with the team is recalled with pride, fun and warmth.

Whakapapa.

## A hyper-local future

My career with Accenture was in two distinct parts. The first thirteen years as a member, and then leader, of the India HR team were in stark contrast to the following fifteen years as I led large, globally-dispersed teams of HR professionals. My transition from a local to a global leader was coincidental with moving from India to Singapore. After being constantly surrounded by peers, employees and team members, my interactions became virtual. Even the person I reported to resided in Chicago and a fourteen-hour time difference separated us for most of the year. A few months after I had moved to Singapore, the global financial crisis struck, precipitating a large scale transformation in HR and, soon, I was asked to lead a high-intensity global program. I started working through the day: mornings and late nights with people in the USA, daytime with people in Asia and Europe. I travelled extensively. I was often on the world's longest flight from Singapore to New York. This continued for seven years as my responsibilities continued to grow. Without realizing it, I was burning out.

Being an Asia-based global leader was atypical, but the travel was not. Many executives fared worse, often calling themselves "road warriors". Looking back, it is mind-numbing to think of the fallout from that lifestyle: the colossal carbon footprint, millions of wasted hours in commute, billions of travel dollars. And then, the incalculable impact on health due to constant disruptions in sleep patterns and poor food habits.

Then came the pandemic that proved to be the proverbial end-of-the-road for globetrotters. One senseless existence of endless travel gave way to another one, this time of unending video calls. We learnt that work could be done from home, that every hour could be a "productive" one, and home could be anywhere. The compounding effect was expanded work hours, lack of human contact and, unsurprisingly, a spike in mental illness that has not yet gone down.

As life returns to the pre-pandemic normalcy, there is a new debate over remote, hybrid, and in-person business models. CEOs and employees find themselves at odds with each other, struggling to triangulate productivity, organizational culture and personal preferences. Tussles are settled, as always, not by sound argument, but by the relative bargaining power of either side, driven by the economics of the labor market.

It is time we stopped debating what is and started creating what may be.

The pandemic precipitated a large-scale, though somewhat short-lived, migration away from metropolitan cities around the world. As people learnt to work from home, they also realized that home could be anywhere. For many who had moved to cities for work, it was now isolating to be away from their families and friends. They yearned for familiarity and security during one of the scariest times in modern history. A year into this new reality, my colleagues in Accenture India did a fun little exercise to map where our nearly 200,000 technology professionals were logging into work each day. The results were astounding. The employee footprint literally covered every square inch of the map, barring inaccessible regions like the Thar desert and extreme ranges in the Himalayas. Consequently, this study informed our location strategy and we opened offices in smaller towns which had a moderate concentration of employees. The new office locations were greeted with cheers as several people opted to move back to their home towns. Employee attrition too came down as a result. These new offices will never be the scale of the ones in the big metros. But they will foster meaningful connection among people with shared roots and benefit the local ecosystem as ancillary industries sprout around them.

Rather than bring people to the office, it is time we took the office to people. Admittedly, this would be easier to orchestrate in early-stage, smaller companies than in large-scale, multi-nationals with capital deployed in expensive office spaces around the world. It would also pose challenges in disseminating the corporate culture, and might restrict the visibility and movement of high potential future leaders. Yet, with a thoughtful strategy that is more like a dial rather than an on-off switch, it is possible to effect such a shift, even in large, established organizations. Local teams will always be an integral part of the overarching corporate structure, and participate in relevant information flows across the matrix. Employees who desire global careers will have opportunities to move and experience different cultures. Importantly, it will build stronger connections among people, create more equity in the society, and bring down the carbon footprint.

A hyper-local future is about eradicating the pandemic of isolation and ushering in the warmth and joy of meaningful, in-person relationships.

## Re-imagining the office

The office should no longer be a place for work.

At least not work in the traditional sense of the word. A core principle for the office of the future is that it ought not be designed for anything that can be done from home. In the knowledge economy employing an estimated one billion workers, this basically means work that can be done by a person on a screen, not requiring much involvement of others, may be conducted at home or other places of a person's choosing, and does not warrant employees coming into an office.

Why should people expend precious resources, time, money and fuel, to do in the office what they can accomplish at home?

That does not mean that we do not need an office. We will absolutely need offices in the future, but the third and final shift is one of fundamentally re-imagining the purpose and nature of the office.

1. For creativity and collaboration. Desks (most, perhaps not all) and cubicles (yes, including those in the fabled corner) need to give way to common ideation spaces, with abundance of markers, whiteboards, stickies, sketchbooks, alongside technology for connecting with others virtually. Learning occurs with and from each other, woven into the fabric of interactions.
2. For celebration. Hyper-local teams, who have shared goals and incentives, meet often to celebrate team milestones and individual accomplishments. There is nothing quite like the energy of a group that takes pride and joy in the accomplishments of its members. A team that celebrates together stays together.
3. For communion. The office is where people connect with their own and other teams. Where local meets global. It is where people see their imprint on the broader organization and form connections with the local community. A place of shared stories.

A distinguishing characteristic of tribes that weathered adversity and survived through the ages, from the many that were vanquished, is a tradition of storytelling. Since time immemorial, they have gathered in the evenings, huddled around fires and shady banyan trees, helping those in need, settling disputes, and when the time came, steeling their resolve to fight their enemies.

May the office be such a sanctuary for connection, creation and communion.
